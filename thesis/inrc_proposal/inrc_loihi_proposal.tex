{\large\bfseries A Spiking State-Space Detector for Event Cameras on Intel Loihi 2}\\[3pt]
\small Dr Will Midgley (PI) \& Benas Vaiciulis (student researcher) \,$\cdot$\, UNSW Sydney\\[2pt]
\small\textbf{Request:} Neuromorphic Research Cloud (vLab) access.

\vspace{-2pt}
\hrule
\vspace{4pt}

\textbf{Objective.} Run a spiking state-space object detector for event cameras on Intel Loihi 2, aiming
at the tight power budget of micro-UAVs, and measure what spiking costs in accuracy and what the chip gives back in
energy and latency.

%---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Motivation}
On Prophesee Gen1, the standard event-camera detection benchmark, spiking detectors now match non-spiking ones:
HsVT~[2] reports up to 47.8 mAP and SpikeDet~[3] up to 47.6, against 47.7 for the non-spiking S5-RVT~[1]. The case
for neuromorphic hardware therefore rests on energy and latency. What remains unmeasured is where the
accuracy cost of spiking comes from. Existing detectors report at most one spiking-vs-non-spiking difference~[4,\,5];
none splits it into its parts, and none uses a selective state-space (Mamba) temporal core.

%---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{What is already built}
We reproduced S5-RVT exactly (47.7 mAP) and built two non-spiking detectors as controlled swaps into the same
pipeline (same neck, head, loss, data and evaluator):

\vspace{-6pt}
PureSSM is the smallest and cheapest model and the most robust to event-rate change: it keeps 69.7\,\% of
its accuracy when the event rate rises tenfold (S5-RVT: 62.2\,\%). Latency and energy are full-pipeline streaming
measurements on an idle-GPU-guarded harness; they are the GPU reference that a
Loihi 2 deployment has to beat.

\textbf{The spiking model is built and trained in simulation.} A leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) readout, trained
with a surrogate gradient, is added after each of PureSSM's Mamba temporal blocks; the spatial backbone stays
non-spiking (a hybrid design, like HsVT~[2]). Three readouts share the same LIF neuron, analog,
graded and binary, so comparing them separates the cost of the neuron dynamics, of sparsity and of
binarisation. On a 100k-step Gen1 run the binary readout reaches 41.8 validation AP, with every layer active
throughout; at the same step the graded readout leads the binary one (41.6 vs 40.4 at 80k). These runs use a
shorter schedule than the table above, so the numbers are not directly comparable; the full ladder is completing.

%---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Why Loihi 2}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{At its core, our temporal block is a diagonal linear recurrence}, which Loihi 2's programmable
  neurons~[7] can implement directly; its projections map to ordinary synaptic layers. Meyer \emph{et al.}~[6] ran a diagonal state-space model on Loihi 2 with about
  $1000\times$ lower energy and $75\times$ lower latency than on a Jetson Orin Nano for streaming input.
  \item \textbf{Event sparsity is wasted on a GPU.} We measured no GPU speed-up from sparse event input; on an
  event-driven chip, idle pixels cost no energy~[8]. This is the long-term case for a fully spiking detector.
  \item \textbf{Graded spikes} on Loihi 2 carry an integer value~[7], the hardware match for our graded readout.
\end{enumerate}

%---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Plan}
Mamba recomputes its decay from the input at every step, whereas the Loihi port in~[6] uses fixed dynamics. We will
therefore either compute the decay on-chip or retrain a fixed-step variant; our rate study suggests a fixed step
need not cost robustness. We train in PyTorch and export through \textbf{NIR}, the vendor-neutral neuromorphic
format. Since the Lava repositories were archived in May 2026, NIR lets the model move to Intel's successor SDK;
we welcome guidance on Intel's preferred path.

\textbf{What the vLab benchmark measures (M3).} The part mapped to the chip is the spiking temporal path
(2.2\,M of PureSSM's 16.3\,M parameters); the spatial backbone stays on a conventional accelerator. M3 asks three
questions: (1) does the exported model give the same detections as the PyTorch model, measured as Gen1 mAP;
(2) what are the energy and latency per frame of the temporal path on Loihi 2, compared with the same path on the
GPU above; and (3) how do these change with the firing threshold, which trades accuracy against spike activity.
We state the scope plainly: the temporal path carries a small share of the detector's compute (spikes feed about 1\,\% of its
operations), so M3 measures the cost and benefit of the temporal block on Loihi 2, not a whole-system energy saving.
That saving needs the spatial backbone to spike as well; a positive M3 is what would justify that step and any
later hardware request.

\section{Risks and mitigations}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.36\textwidth} Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Risk} & \textbf{Mitigation} \\
\midrule
Mamba's input-dependent decay has no direct Loihi primitive. & Retrain a fixed-step variant through the same
pipeline and measure what it costs in accuracy and rate robustness. \\
Recurrent state is large: 144\,MB per stream for the whole model (fp32), likely more than one chip's memory. &
Map the deepest, smallest stages first; reduce the state size or precision; spread across several chips. \\
Spike activity is high: firing rates of 0.36--0.48 during training. & The threshold is already a tunable
parameter; a threshold sweep gives the energy--accuracy trade-off directly. \\
Lava is archived and its successor has no release date. & NIR export keeps the model independent of the SDK. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
